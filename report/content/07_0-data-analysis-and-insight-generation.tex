\section{Outlier Detection}
To assess the presence of anomalous rating values, I examined the distribution of 
white player Elo ratings shown in Figure~\ref{fig:white_elo_distribution}. The 
histogram reveals a plausible range from approximately 800 to 2400, with no 
observations falling below 800 or exceeding 2400. Ratings below 200 or above 2800 
are rare but entirely possible in a real-world chess dataset. They correspond to 
absolute beginners and titled grandmasters, respectively. Because these values 
reflect genuine player skill levels rather than data entry errors or measurement 
artefacts, I retained them without modification. No further outlier removal or 
capping was applied, ensuring that the subsequent machine learning pipeline 
operates on authentic distributional characteristics.

Figure~\ref{fig:rating_by_outcome} presents the rating distribution of all players

distribution centred around approximately 2000 Elo. Together, these figures 
confirm that the dataset contains meaningful rating signals and that no 
systematic data collection error distorts the relationship between rating and 
outcome. Because extreme ratings (below 800 or above 2800) are rare but valid, 
they were retained as authentic representatives of beginner and master-level play.

\section{Relationships and Patterns}

Figure~\ref{fig:rating_diff_winprob} illustrates the relationship between the Elo 
rating difference (White Elo minus Black Elo) and the observed probability of a 
White win. The rating difference axis is discretised into bins, with negative 
values indicating a Black advantage and positive values a White advantage. The 
resulting curve follows a clear logistic shape: when White is significantly 
weaker (rating difference below -600), the win probability falls below 0.2; 
conversely, when White is stronger by more than +600 points, the win probability 
rises above 0.8. The curve passes through approximately 0.5 when the rating 
difference is near zero, as expected for equally matched players. This pattern 
validates the fundamental assumption of the Elo rating system---that the expected 
score (win probability) is a logistic function of the rating gap. For my 
predictive model, this strong monotonic relationship confirms that \texttt{rating\_diff} 
will be one of the most influential features, and it also provides a baseline 
against which to compare the model's learned decision boundary.

Figure~\ref{fig:termination_by_time} examines how game termination type varies 
with time control. In bullet games ($\le$ 3 minutes), time forfeits account for 
approximately 40\% of all terminations, while normal terminations (checkmate or 
resignation) constitute the remaining 60\%. This reflects the extreme time 
pressure in bullet chess, where players frequently run out of time before a 
decisive result occurs on the board. In contrast, for rapid and longer time 
controls (>10 minutes), the proportion of time forfeits drops dramatically to 
below 10\%, with normal terminations dominating the outcome. This pattern is 
intuitive: more thinking time reduces the likelihood of flagging. From an 
MLOps perspective, this relationship suggests that \texttt{time\_control} is a valuable 
predictor of termination type, and models that ignore this feature would 
generalise poorly across different game formats. The pipeline could also use 
this insight to trigger different post-processing logic (e.g., predicting 
time-forfeit probability separately for bullet games).

\section{Dimension Tables Overview}

Figure~\ref{fig:describe_other_tables} presents the schema of the three dimension tables 
that complement the central \texttt{fact\_games} table in my star schema. 

The \texttt{dim\_player} table stores information about each unique chess player 
identified by a \texttt{player\_id}. The \texttt{username} column holds the player's Lichess 
handle, while \texttt{title} (e.g., FM, IM, GM) records any official title. 
\texttt{last\_known\_elo} is a slowly changing attribute that captures the player's most 
recent rating; this approach avoids updating every historical game record when a 
rating changes.

The \texttt{dim\_opening} table maps each \texttt{opening\_id} to its \texttt{eco} code (e.g., B30, C50) 
and a human-readable \texttt{opening\_name}. This separation allows the fact table to 
reference only the compact opening identifier, saving space and enabling 
consistency across millions of games.

Finally, \texttt{dim\_date} provides a time dimension with \texttt{date\_id} (a surrogate key), 
\texttt{calendar\_date}, \texttt{year}, \texttt{month}, and \texttt{day\_of\_week}. This design supports time-based 
analyses such as monthly win-rate trends or day-of-week performance comparisons 
without repeatedly storing full date strings in the fact table.

Together, these dimension tables enforce referential integrity and greatly 
improve query performance for analytical workloads, as joins are performed only 
on compact integer or short string keys.

\section{Missing Values Analysis}
Figure~\ref{fig:missing_values} presents the results of a missing value audit 
across key tables. In the \texttt{fact\_games} table, only 218 out of 121,332 rows 
(approximately 0.18\%) have a missing white or black Elo rating. This extremely 
low proportion indicates that rating data is almost always present for rated 
games, which is expected given Lichess's data collection practices. These missing 
rows are likely due to unrated games or very old PGN files where ratings were not 
recorded. Because the percentage is negligible, I chose to drop these rows 
during model training rather than impute them, as their removal does not 
meaningfully affect statistical power.

In contrast, the batch predictions table shows that all 4,400 rows have a 
\texttt{NULL} value in the player's elo column. This is not a data error but rather a 
design choice in my inference pipeline. The model does not require player-specific 
ratings as input features when generating predictions from the batch evaluation 
script. The \texttt{player\_elo} column was retained in the schema for potential future 
use (e.g., personalised predictions) but was left intentionally empty for the 
current batch run. Therefore, no remediation is needed. For production 
deployment, I would either populate this column with the relevant player 
ratings or remove the column entirely to avoid confusion.

\section{Model Drift}

Deploying machine learning models into production environments exposes them to dynamic real-world conditions where the underlying data generating processes are rarely stationary~\cite{gama2014}. This non-stationarity leads to a progressive decline in predictive accuracy over time, a phenomenon commonly termed \textit{model drift}, \textit{model decay}, or \textit{predictive model decay}~\cite{ibm_drift}. According to research from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, approximately seventy percent of deployed machine learning models experience significant performance degradation within their first year of operation, with systemic shifts in data quality and environment semantics acting as primary drivers rather than algorithmic failure~\cite{countly}. Within an enterprise-grade MLOps pipeline designed to process massive game dumps from platforms such as \texttt{lichess.database.org}, detecting and interpreting these shifts is critical to maintaining the validity of ratings, player behaviour classifications, and automated cheat-detection systems~\cite{lichess2025}.

% To mathematically characterise the operational environment, the data-generating process at any given temporal point \(t\) is defined as a joint probability distribution \(P_{t}(X, Y)\) over the input covariate space \(X\) and the target output space \(Y\)~\cite{gama2014}. The joint probability distribution is decomposed into a marginal distribution of the features \(P(X)\) and a conditional distribution of the target given the features \(P(Y|X)\), expressed as \(P(X,Y) = P(X) \cdot P(Y|X)\)~\cite{gama2014}. Model drift occurs when the joint distribution at a specific production time step \(t+1\) deviates from the baseline training distribution at time \(t\), such that \(P_{t+1}(X,Y) \neq P_{t}(X,Y)\)~\cite{webb2018}. This overall distribution shift is segmented into three distinct statistical phenomena~\cite{gama2014, webb2018}:

% \begin{itemize}
%     \item \textbf{Covariate Shift (Virtual Drift)}: This shift occurs when the marginal distribution of the input features changes over time, while the conditional probability mapping features to target labels remains stationary, written as \(P_{t+1}(X) \neq P_{t}(X)\) and \(P_{t+1}(Y|X) = P_{t}(Y|X)\)~\cite{shimodaira2000}. Formulated initially by Shimodaira in 2000, covariate shift assumes that the training data and operational test data are drawn from different distributions, yet the underlying decision rules remain constant~\cite{shimodaira2000}. For example, if a chess platform experiences a massive influx of novice players, the marginal distribution of user ratings \(P(X)\) shifts significantly downward, yet the underlying relationship between rating differences and game outcomes \(P(Y|X)\) remains invariant~\cite{microsoft2022}.

%     \item \textbf{Real Concept Drift (Posterior Shift)}: This shift occurs when the relationship between the input features and the target variable changes over time, regardless of any changes in the marginal distribution of the covariates, written as \(P_{t+1}(Y|X) \neq P_{t}(Y|X)\) and \(P_{t+1}(X) = P_{t}(X)\)~\cite{webb2018}. This represents a physical alteration of the optimal decision boundary~\cite{webb2018}. In a chess analytics context, if a server-side engine update or major patch modifies the automated positional assessment of board states, a specific feature vector \(x\) representing a pawn structure may now map to a different victory probability \(P(Y|x)\), shifting \(P(Y|X)\) without altering the frequency of the board states themselves~\cite{ibm_drift}.

%     \item \textbf{Prior Probability Shift (Label Drift)}: This shift manifests when the marginal distribution of the target variable changes over time while the class-conditional distributions remain constant, written as \(P_{t+1}(Y) \neq P_{t}(Y)\) and \(P_{t+1}(X|Y) = P_{t}(X|Y)\)~\cite{webb2018}. This occurs when the overall proportion of class outcomes changes~\cite{webb2018}. For instance, a sudden surge in player reports or flaggings during a seasonal competitive event skew the baseline expectation of the proportion of anomalous accounts, requiring models to adjust classification thresholds to prevent false positives~\cite{lichess2025}.

\subsection{Concept Drift}
Webb's taxonomy categorises concept drift into four temporal profiles~\cite{webb2018}. Sudden drift replaces the old distribution instantaneously, as when a rating system update shifts all historical ratings~\cite{ibm_drift}. Gradual drift involves coexistence of old and new concepts over time, such as players slowly migrating to a new mobile app~\cite{ibm_drift}. Incremental drift is a continuous stepwise evolution, like the gradual inflation of ratings over years~\cite{webb2018}. Recurring drift sees concepts disappear and reappear irregularly, typical of seasonal playing patterns~\cite{webb2018}. A major practical challenge is the delayed labelling problem: ground truth (e.g., cheating verdicts) may arrive hours or weeks after predictions, breaking supervised detectors that assume immediate labels~\cite{webb2018}.

\subsection{Drift Mitigation Strategies for the Pipeline}

For protecting against concept drift, the following strategies can be employed for production deployment:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Periodic retraining}: Retrain the model weekly on the most recent month of data to adapt to gradual shifts.
    \item \textbf{Statistical drift detection}: Use the Population Stability Index (PSI) on \texttt{rating\_diff} and \texttt{time\_control\_seconds} with an alert threshold of PSI \(> 0.2\).
    \item \textbf{Performance monitoring}: Track accuracy and AUC on a rolling window of predictions with known outcomes (from \texttt{batch\_predictions}). A drop of more than 5\% triggers an alert.
    \item \textbf{Explainability auditing}: Apply SHAP explanations periodically to check whether feature importances remain stable.
\end{enumerate}
